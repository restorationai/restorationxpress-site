## Should You Call a Plumber or a Restoration Company First?

Call whichever can stop the problem fastest, then call the other within the hour. A plumber if water is still actively leaking from the supply line, P-trap, garbage disposal gasket, or dishwasher hose. A restoration company if the leak has already soaked the cabinet base, toe kick, subfloor, or the wall behind the sink.

In practice, most under-sink calls need both. The plumber replaces the cracked PEX line, re-seats the trap, or tightens the dishwasher fitting. That's a repair, not a drying job, and most plumbers don't carry the extraction and dehumidification equipment to dry a cabinet box or a wet subfloor to the [ANSI/IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) standard. A restoration company picks up from there: pulling baseboards if needed, running moisture readings, and setting drying equipment until the materials are back to a dry standard, not just dry to the touch.

If the leak looks small and you can see exactly where it's coming from, it's fine to shut the valve under the sink yourself and call the plumber first. If you're not sure how long the water has been sitting, call the restoration company first so moisture mapping starts before more material is affected.

## What Happens During the First Visit?

A technician checks how far the water traveled using a moisture meter, not just how wet things look on the surface. Cabinet sides, the subfloor directly under the sink base, and the wall cavity behind the plumbing often hold moisture long after the counter looks dry.

Have a few things ready before the crew arrives: the location of the shut-off valve (so it's confirmed closed), your homeowners insurance policy number, and photos of the standing water and open cabinet taken before anything is moved. The technician will typically pull the kick plate and any removable cabinet panels, check the subfloor for soft spots or delamination, and set air movers and a dehumidifier to dry the cavity over the following one to three days, depending on how much the materials absorbed.

## Can Swollen Particleboard Cabinets Be Saved?

Solid wood cabinet boxes often dry out fine if the water is caught and the area is dried within 24 to 48 hours. Particleboard or MDF cabinet bases, which is what most stock kitchen cabinets under the sink are built from, swell permanently once they've absorbed water and rarely return to their original shape.

That means the cabinet face and sides might be salvageable even when the base needs to be cut out and replaced. A restoration estimate should separate what can be dried and kept from what needs to come out, rather than treating the whole cabinet as a loss. The subfloor underneath gets the same scrutiny: plywood that's delaminating or showing a soft, spongy feel under moisture readings usually gets cut back to dry, sound material before anything is rebuilt.

## Could There Be Mold Behind the Toe Kick?

Mold can start growing on wet particleboard or drywall within 24 to 48 hours of a leak, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home). That means a leak that sat unnoticed behind a cabinet for a few days, which is common since the toe kick hides it from view, can already have mold growth before anyone finds the puddle.

Davie's year-round humidity makes this worse. Even after visible water is gone, moisture trapped inside a cabinet side panel or behind the toe kick keeps that microclimate damp longer than a drier inland location would. If you see dark spotting, smell a musty odor near the sink, or the drying crew finds elevated moisture behind a wall after extraction, that's a signal for [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) rather than just more drying time.

## How Do You Document This for a Water Damage Claim?

Photograph the pooling water, the open cabinet, and anything visibly damaged before cleanup starts, then keep the plumber's invoice and the restoration company's moisture readings as your claim's paper trail. Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a supply line that bursts without warning, but they typically exclude damage from a leak that was gradual or went unaddressed for a long stretch, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance). Coverage always depends on the specific policy language, so don't assume either way until you've read it or asked your adjuster directly.

If the source turns out to be a slowly weeping connection rather than a sudden failure, say so honestly. A documented, prompt response to stop further damage protects the claim regardless of which category the original leak falls into.
