## What drives the price on a commercial water damage job?

Square footage and water category set the baseline, but five other factors shift the final invoice up or down on a commercial loss. Understanding them helps you sanity-check a scope before you sign it.

- **Water category.** A clean supply line break is Category 1 and the least expensive to remediate. Water from an AC condensate overflow or a washing machine discharge is Category 2 (gray water) and costs more because more material gets removed rather than dried in place. Any sewer or drain backup, no matter how it looks, is Category 3 (black water) under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) and requires antimicrobial treatment and PPE, which raises labor cost.
- **Response time.** Water sitting for 24 to 48 hours lets porous materials saturate further and mold begin to establish, which usually means more demolition and a bigger invoice. Calling in the first few hours after discovery keeps the job closer to drying in place.
- **Business interruption needs.** If the space can't close, crews often work nights or in phases around operating hours, which costs more than a straightforward daytime job in a vacant unit.
- **Tenant coordination.** In multi-tenant buildings near Pine Island Ridge or along the busier commercial corridors of Davie, drying equipment and containment have to be scheduled around multiple leases, not just one business.
- **Documentation requirements.** Commercial carriers typically require daily moisture readings and drying logs, not just a final report. That documentation takes technician time, but it's also what gets a commercial claim approved without delay.

## Does commercial insurance cover water damage in Florida?

Most commercial property policies cover sudden, accidental water damage from a covered cause, like a burst supply line or a sprinkler malfunction, the same way a homeowners policy would. What they typically exclude is flood water, gradual leaks, and lost income unless you've added business income coverage.

Flood from outside the building, storm surge, or rising groundwater is not covered by a standard commercial property policy; that requires a separate flood policy, either through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood carrier. Gradual leaks and deferred maintenance are also usually denied, so if a slow roof leak has been dripping for months, don't expect the same response as a burst pipe claim.

Business income coverage, which pays for lost revenue while the space is unusable, is a separate endorsement most policies don't include by default. If downtime is a real risk for your operation, check whether that rider is on your policy now, not after a loss. For documentation that actually holds up with an adjuster, the Insurance Information Institute's guide on [settling insurance claims after a disaster](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) is a useful baseline, and our own breakdown of [water damage restoration costs in Davie](/blog/water-damage-restoration-cost/) covers how the same documentation principles apply to residential claims.

## What happens during a commercial water damage restoration job?

A commercial job follows the same core process as a residential one, scaled up for square footage and tenant coordination. Here's the sequence:

1. **Assessment and category classification.** A technician identifies the water source, classifies it as Category 1, 2, or 3 under the IICRC S500 standard, and maps the affected square footage.
2. **Extraction.** Truck-mounted or commercial-grade extractors pull standing water from carpet, carpet tile, or hard flooring. Larger floor plans may need multiple extraction units running at once.
3. **Containment and demolition where needed.** Saturated drywall, ceiling tile, or insulation that can't be dried in place gets removed along a flood-cut line, more aggressively on Category 2 and 3 losses.
4. **Structural drying.** Commercial-grade air movers and LGR or desiccant dehumidifiers are sized to the cubic footage of the space, not just the square footage, since open ceilings and large floor plans hold more airborne moisture.
5. **Daily monitoring and drying logs.** Technicians take moisture readings on a schedule and log them, which is the documentation most commercial carriers require before they'll approve the claim.
6. **Final moisture verification.** Once materials read dry to the standard, the technician documents final readings to close out the scope with the carrier.
