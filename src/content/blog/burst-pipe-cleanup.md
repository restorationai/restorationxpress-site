## What happens during professional burst pipe cleanup?

Burst pipe cleanup is a five-step sequence: extraction, moisture mapping, selective demolition, structural drying, and clearance testing. Skipping any one of them is how a "dried" house still grows mold in the walls six weeks later.

A technician starts with truck-mounted or portable extraction to pull standing and absorbed water out of carpet, pad, and flooring. Then comes moisture mapping: a penetrating moisture meter checks drywall, baseboards, subfloor, and cabinetry to find exactly how far the water traveled, including into wall cavities you can't see. Only after that mapping is done does anyone decide what gets torn out versus dried in place.

Drying equipment, air movers and low-grain-refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers, goes in next, sized to the square footage and the amount of wet material, not a flat number of units per room. The crew returns daily (or every other day) to re-check meter readings and adjust equipment. Drying ends when readings match the dry standard set by the unaffected areas of the home, not a fixed number of days.

## How long does burst pipe cleanup take?

Most single-room losses from a clean-water pipe break dry in three to five days with equipment running continuously. That's the realistic range for a bathroom or kitchen where water stayed on tile or hard flooring and didn't soak deep into framing.

The timeline stretches when water got into wall cavities, under cabinetry, or beneath hardwood. Expect five to ten days if drywall needs to stay open for air movement, or if the loss hit more than one room. Older homes in neighborhoods like Pine Island Ridge or Rolling Hills, where plumbing and subfloor materials have had decades to age, sometimes run longer because water wicks further into porous materials before anyone notices the leak.

Two things stretch the timeline beyond the equipment itself: delayed response (water that sat for 48+ hours before anyone called for help wicks further and takes longer to pull back out) and insurance approval delays if an adjuster needs to inspect before demo starts.

## Which materials get torn out, and which get dried in place?

The decision comes down to the water's category and how long the material was saturated, not a blanket rule. Clean water (Category 1) from a supply-line break can often be dried in place if addressed within 24 to 48 hours; water that sat longer, or came from a fixture that backs up with contaminants, usually requires removal.

Drywall below the waterline typically comes out if it was submerged or wicking for more than a day or two, since wet gypsum board holds moisture a dehumidifier can't fully pull out and provides a food source for mold. Baseboards almost always come off, since they trap water behind them even after the visible surface looks dry. Solid hardwood often has to be removed and replaced if it cupped; engineered flooring and tile usually survive if dried quickly. Insulation in exterior walls is rarely salvageable once wet; it holds water and loses its R-value.

This is also where the IICRC's [S500 water damage restoration standard](https://www.iicrc.org/page/IICRCS500) matters in practice: it's the industry reference crews use to classify the water and decide drying versus removal, rather than guessing room by room.
