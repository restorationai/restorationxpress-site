What you do in the next ten minutes matters more than who you eventually call. Shut off the water, figure out what kind of water you're dealing with, then decide. Here's how to work through that in order.

## Who do you call first, a plumber or a restoration company?
Call a plumber first only if water is still actively flowing and won't stop at the shutoff valve, or if the overflow is caused by a clog deep in the line. Call a water damage restoration company once the water is stopped and you're looking at what it left behind.

Most Davie homes on slab foundations with tile or vinyl bathroom flooring handle a one-time overflow fine if the water is caught fast. The plumber fixes why it happened. The restoration side handles the drying, the moisture readings in the subfloor and walls, and the documentation your insurer will ask for. If both are needed, it's normal to have a plumber clear the line the same afternoon a drying crew sets up equipment.

## How do you stop a toilet overflow at the source?
Every toilet has a shutoff valve on the wall or floor behind it, usually a small oval handle on a supply line. Turn it clockwise until it stops; that cuts the water regardless of what's happening in the bowl.

If you can't find the valve or it's seized, shut off the main water supply to the house instead. Don't keep flushing to try to clear a clog. Each flush adds more water to a floor that's already wet, and if the clog is downstream, it pushes contaminated water higher into the bowl.

## Is the overflow water actually dangerous?
That depends entirely on where the water came from, not how much of it there is. The [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) splits water losses into three categories, and a toilet overflow can land in any of them.

If the overflow came from a supply-line failure above the bowl, before it ever touched waste, that's Category 1, clean water. It's still worth drying fast, but it's generally safe to clean yourself. If the bowl itself overflowed from ordinary use, urine but no feces, that's Category 2, gray water, and it calls for gloves, a disinfectant cleaner, and more caution with anything porous it touched. If water came up through the drain from below, carries feces, or smells like sewage, treat it as Category 3, black water. Don't clean that yourself. It's a contamination hazard that needs a specialist equipped for sewage-level contamination, not a mop and a bottle of bleach.

## What happens to the floor and walls if it's not dried fast?
Tile and vinyl bathroom floors look fine on the surface while the subfloor underneath them stays wet for days. Plywood subfloor soaks up water through the seams and can swell, delaminate, or stay damp long enough to feed [mold growth within 24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold), even though the floor above it looks dry to the touch.

Drywall does the same thing sideways: it wicks water up from a wet baseboard the way a paper towel pulls water up a glass, often a foot or more above the visible waterline. That's why a restoration crew uses a moisture meter and sometimes a thermal camera instead of eyeballing it. If the reading comes back wet, the drywall gets cut and dried or replaced, not just dried on the surface. ## When is it fine to just clean it up yourself?
A small, clean-water overflow caught within minutes, with no standing water under the vanity or baseboards, is usually a DIY job: towels, a wet vac if you have one, a fan, and keeping an eye on it for a day or two. If the water sat for more than an hour, soaked under cabinetry, or you're not sure which category it was, that's the point to call in a professional rather than guess.

## Will homeowners insurance cover this?
Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, which a toilet overflow usually qualifies as, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance). What they typically don't cover is damage from a backed-up sewer or drain line unless you've added a water backup endorsement, so check your policy before assuming a Category 3 backup is covered.

Photograph the standing water, the affected walls and flooring, and anything damaged before cleanup starts, and keep any receipts from towels, a rented fan, or a plumber's invoice. A written moisture log from a restoration technician also helps if the adjuster questions how far the water traveled, and if the damage was significant enough to affect air quality, drying it properly reduces the odds you'll need [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) down the line.
