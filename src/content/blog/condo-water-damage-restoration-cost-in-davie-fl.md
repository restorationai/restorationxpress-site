If brown water is spreading across your dining room ceiling in a Pine Island Ridge condo, or your downstairs neighbor near Nova Southeastern University just texted you a photo of water dripping into their kitchen, the first question isn't just "how much will this cost." It's "whose policy pays for which part." Condo losses split differently than a single-family home in Rolling Hills or Shenandoah: the extraction and drying bill might land on your HO6 policy, the drywall behind the wall might belong to the association's master policy, and if water reached the unit below, you're now managing two policies and two approvals at once.

## How much does condo water damage restoration cost in Davie?

A typical condo water loss in Davie runs from roughly $1,500 for a contained, single-room mitigation job to $25,000 or more when the leak reaches the unit below and both drywall and flooring need replacing. The final number depends on how many units are affected, how long the water sat before anyone called, and whether cabinets or flooring need full replacement rather than drying in place.

## What makes a condo water loss cost more than a single-family home?

Three things drive the price up in a condo that a single-family home in Cooper City or Weston usually doesn't have to deal with: shared walls and floors that let water migrate into a neighbor's unit, association rules that can delay the start of work, and after-hours building access that often means calling a property manager before anyone can get a key.

Shared construction is the biggest cost multiplier. A leak that stays contained to one room in a detached home can travel through a shared slab or wall assembly in a Pine Island Ridge mid-rise and show up as a stain on the ceiling one floor down. That turns a single mitigation job into two, each with its own drying equipment, its own documentation, and often its own insurance adjuster.

Access and approval add time, and time adds cost. Many Davie condo and townhome associations require a property manager or board sign-off before a contractor can bring equipment into common hallways or run hoses through a shared corridor. A crew that could start extraction the same hour in a single-family home may need to wait for that approval first, which lets Category 1 clean water sit longer and raises the odds that porous materials need full removal instead of drying in place.

## What does your HO6 policy pay for, and what does the association's master policy cover?

Your HO6 condo policy typically pays for the inside of your unit, your personal belongings, and any loss assessment the association bills you after a shared loss; Florida's condominium law (Chapter 718) generally requires the association's master policy to cover the building's original structure and common elements. The split between the two is where most disputes start.

In practice, that usually means your HO6 covers paint, flooring you installed, cabinets, and contents, while the master policy covers the drywall, insulation, and structural elements the developer originally installed. Many HO6 policies also include loss assessment coverage, a rider that reimburses you if the association bills unit owners for a shared deductible or an uninsured portion of a common-area loss. It's worth confirming that rider is on your policy before you need it. For the Florida-specific rules on which party is responsible in different scenarios, our [Who Pays for Water Damage in a Condo](/blog/who-pays-for-water-damage-in-a-condo/) guide walks through the statute in more detail. For general background on what a standard policy is built to cover, see the [Insurance Information Institute's guide to homeowners coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).

Document everything from the first phone call: photos of the ceiling stain, the source of the water, and the date it was first noticed. Both your HO6 carrier and the association's insurer will ask for this, and prompt mitigation protects your claim either way.

The process starts with a moisture assessment, moves into extraction and drying, and ends with documentation both insurers can use to settle their share of the claim. Each phase is tracked so the paperwork matches what actually happened in the unit.

2. **Extraction and containment.** Standing water is removed and containment is set up if the leak has reached a shared wall or the unit below.
3. **Monitored drying.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run on a schedule, with daily moisture readings logged to the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for professional water damage restoration.
4. **Documentation for both policies.** Photos, moisture logs, and a written scope separate what belongs to your HO6 claim from what belongs to the association's master policy.

6. **Final walkthrough.** Moisture readings confirm the space is dry before reconstruction is signed off.
