## When Do You Call a Plumber vs. a Restoration Company?

Call a plumber first only if water is actively flowing from a source you can't shut off yourself; call a restoration crew at the same time, because extraction needs to start before standing water soaks into subflooring and baseboards.

Most kitchen leaks have a shutoff close by: the ice machine's supply valve, the dishwasher's inline valve, the isolation valve behind the three-compartment sink, or the main water shutoff if you can't find the specific line. If you can safely reach it, shut it off. If the leak is from a water heater, a damaged supply line inside a wall, or you simply can't locate the shutoff, that's a job for an emergency plumber, not a server on a step stool.

If water is near an electrical panel, under equipment with exposed wiring, or you see sparking, don't wade in. Cut power at the breaker only if you can do it without stepping in water, and call an electrician before anyone goes back in. For everything else, structural drying, moisture mapping, and getting the dining room usable again fall under [water damage restoration](/services/water-damage-restoration/), and the sooner that starts, the fewer days you're closed.

## What If the Water Is Contaminated (Grease Trap or Drain Backup)?

Water backing up from a grease trap, floor drain, or sewer line is Category 3 water under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), meaning it's treated as grossly contaminated and most of what it touches in a food-service kitchen can't be saved.

That classification matters because it changes the whole response. Any food, single-use packaging, cardboard, or porous material the water contacted gets discarded, not wiped down and shelved. Carpet, baseboards, and lower drywall in the contaminated area typically come out rather than get dried in place. A plumber still needs to clear whatever caused the backup; a restoration crew handles the contaminated-water side, documents the category and extent for your insurer, and tells you plainly what's salvageable versus what isn't. This is different from a clean-water leak off an ice machine line, which is Category 1 at the start and dries out with far less demolition if you move fast, which is exactly why our [mold growth timeline](/blog/mold-growth-timeline/) breakdown matters here: damp drywall and cabinetry in a 75-degree kitchen can start growing mold within 24 to 48 hours, per the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home).

## What Does Davie Require Before You Can Reopen?

Florida restaurants need to pass muster with the [DBPR Division of Hotels and Restaurants](https://www.myfloridalicense.com/DBPR/hotels-restaurants/) whenever water has contacted food, food storage, or food-contact surfaces, and reopening without addressing that can cost you the inspection.

In practice that means: discard any food, ice, or packaging the water touched, no exceptions for sealed containers that were submerged. Clean and sanitize every food-contact surface in the affected area before it's used again. If the health department or a routine inspection follows, having dated photos and a written drying log from your restoration company backs up that the space was handled correctly, not just mopped and reopened. A contained leak in a back storage room might let you keep serving from the front of house while drying happens; a flood that reaches the line or walk-in usually means a full closure until drying and cleaning are documented.
