## Restoration emergencies common in Miami Gardens

Miami Gardens sits inland from the coast but still takes the full brunt of South Florida's wet season: afternoon thunderstorms roll in nearly every day from June through September, and tropical systems can stall over Miami-Dade County and drop a season's worth of rain in a weekend. Flat terrain and a high water table mean stormwater has nowhere to go fast, so street flooding and sheet flow toward low-lying slabs are routine. Add year-round humidity in the 70 to 90 percent range and any water intrusion, from a slow roof leak to a failed water heater, can turn into visible mold growth in as little as 48 hours if it isn't dried properly. Roof damage from wind-driven rain and the occasional direct hurricane hit rounds out the pattern we see most.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Miami Gardens

Much of Miami Gardens was built out from the 1950s through the 1980s, with concrete block and stucco (CBS) construction and slab-on-grade foundations typical of South Florida, since the high water table rules out basements and most crawlspaces. That slab construction means water intrusion tends to spread laterally under flooring before it's visible, which changes how a drying plan has to be scoped. Plumbing from that era is a mix of cast iron drain lines and galvanized or copper supply piping, with some later properties built during the polybutylene years of the 80s and early 90s; both are common points of failure we plan around.

Miami Gardens falls within Miami-Dade County's High-Velocity Hurricane Zone, so structural repairs and rebuilds are held to that stricter wind-load standard, and permits for repair work are pulled through the City of Miami Gardens Building Division. Any scope involving roof, structural, or electrical work should assume a permit step before the job closes out.
