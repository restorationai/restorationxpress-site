A single-room water loss and a large loss event are not the same problem at different volumes. They're different problems. When a loss spans multiple units, multiple floors, or crosses structural and mechanical systems at once, the usual sequence of extract-dry-rebuild breaks down unless someone is coordinating crews, equipment, and documentation across the whole property simultaneously. That's what large loss and catastrophic response actually means: mobilizing enough resources, fast enough, to keep a six or seven-figure loss from compounding while adjusters, property managers, and ownership groups are still assessing the scope.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

Large loss work typically covers events that exceed a defined dollar threshold (commonly $50,000 and up, though carriers vary) or that affect multiple units, buildings, or tenants at once: a burst main in a mid-rise, a commercial roof failure after a storm, a sprinkler malfunction across several floors of office space, or hurricane damage to a multi-building apartment complex. The work itself is the same core restoration discipline scaled up: extraction, structural drying, demolition of unsalvageable materials, and reconstruction. What changes is the logistics. Crews have to be sequenced so drying equipment doesn't overload building power, contents from dozens of units need tracked inventory and pack-out, and reporting has to be granular enough that an adjuster or loss consultant can verify square footage, moisture readings, and labor hours line by line.

## Our process

1. **Rapid scope and mobilization.** A lead estimator walks the property to establish the extent of damage, affected square footage, and immediate safety hazards, then calls in the crew size and equipment the scale actually requires.
2. **Triage and stabilization.** Structural drying and water extraction start in the highest-risk areas first, typically mechanical rooms, lower floors, and anywhere water is migrating into shared walls or ceilings, to stop secondary damage from spreading while the rest of the site is scoped.
3. **Phased extraction and demolition.** Crews work building-by-building or floor-by-floor rather than all at once, so drying equipment placement and power draw stay manageable and progress can be verified area by area.
4. **Daily moisture monitoring and reporting.** Psychrometric readings and drying logs are tracked per affected unit or zone, which is what large loss adjusters and independent loss consultants expect to see before they'll approve the next phase of work.
5. **Reconstruction coordination.** Once drying is verified complete, rebuild scopes are packaged by trade and sequenced so the property can return tenants or operations in stages rather than waiting for the entire site to finish at once.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on large or catastrophic losses isn't bad drying technique, it's bad sequencing and documentation. A contractor who shows up with a standard residential crew and no logistics plan will under-resource the first 48 hours, which is when secondary damage (swelling, mold colonization, electrical hazards) actually gets locked in. Large loss adjusters are looking for daily moisture logs tied to specific rooms or units, photo documentation that matches the line-item estimate, and a clear chain of custody on removed contents, not a lump-sum invoice at the end. Multi-million dollar restoration claims also get bogged down when a single contractor tries to self-perform every trade instead of bringing in qualified subcontractors for specialty work like structural engineering review or large-scale dehumidification, so a response that scales properly usually means a general contractor's reconstruction crew working alongside a dedicated restoration team from day one, not sequentially.
