When a fire, flood, or storm leaves a home with torn-out drywall, charred framing, or saturated subfloor, the demolition and drying crews eventually pack up, and what's left is a structural shell that needs to become a livable space again. That's where reconstruction services in Davie come in: turning a mitigated loss into finished square footage, built back to code and ready for a final walkthrough with your adjuster.

## What reconstruction services actually involves

Post-damage reconstruction picks up where mitigation leaves off. A water loss that required tear-out of wet drywall and flooring needs new framing where studs or sill plates were compromised, new insulation, drywall, texture, paint, trim, and flooring to match. A fire loss often means rebuilding from the studs out in the rooms closest to the point of origin, plus smoke-damaged areas further away that only need paint and trim. Structural reconstruction can involve load-bearing repairs, roof decking replacement, or foundation-adjacent work depending on how deep the damage went.

Timelines vary more here than in almost any other restoration service. A single bathroom rebuild might run two to three weeks once materials are ordered. A whole-floor reconstruction after a major flood can run several months, especially if permitting, material lead times, or insurance approval slow things down. The work itself is construction trade work: framing, electrical rough-in, plumbing rough-in, drywall, finish carpentry, flooring, and paint, sequenced the way any remodel would be, just triggered by a loss instead of a renovation.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and documentation.** We walk the space, photograph existing conditions, and write a line-item scope that matches what mitigation removed and what needs to go back. This document is what your adjuster reviews for approval.
2. **Permits and plan review.** Structural work, electrical, and plumbing typically require permits through the local building department. We pull what's required before framing starts, not after, which avoids failed inspections later.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Studs, subfloor, and roof decking get replaced where they failed moisture or fire testing. Electrical and plumbing rough-in happens before walls close up, and rough-in inspections happen at this stage.
4. **Drywall, texture, and finishing.** Insulation, drywall, taping, texture matching, and paint come next. Matching existing texture and sheen is where a lot of reconstruction jobs show seams if it's rushed.
5. **Flooring, trim, and final walkthrough.** Baseboards, door casing, flooring, and fixtures go in last, followed by a punch list walkthrough with the homeowner before we call the job complete.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point is sequencing drywall before an area has actually dried to the standard the mitigation report specified. If moisture readings aren't verified before close-in, mold or warping shows up behind new drywall within weeks, and the whole wall has to come back out. A second common mistake is skipping permits on work that technically requires them: unpermitted electrical or structural repairs can surface during a future home sale inspection and become the homeowner's problem years later.

Adjusters reviewing a reconstruction estimate are looking for a scope that lines up with the mitigation report, with no padding and no missing line items. They'll flag mismatched material grades (replacing builder-grade flooring with premium flooring, for example) unless that upgrade is itemized separately and the homeowner is covering the difference. A scope that's detailed, photographed, and tied directly to documented damage moves through claims review faster than one that reads like a generic estimate template.

## What does reconstruction cost?

Reconstruction costs scale with square footage, trade complexity (electrical and plumbing rough-in add cost), and finish level. These are typical industry ranges, not a quote: every loss is different, and RestorationXpress provides a written scope of work before any reconstruction begins.

| Scenario | Typical range |

Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction costs tied to a covered peril, such as rebuilding after a burst pipe or fire, though policy limits, depreciation, and code-upgrade coverage all affect what's actually reimbursed.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

South Florida's housing stock is heavy on concrete block stucco (CBS) construction, which behaves differently than wood-frame homes during reconstruction: block walls don't need the same framing repair as wood studs, but stucco patching and color-matching is its own trade skill. Hurricane season brings a spike in roof and structural reconstruction work tied to wind and water intrusion, and material lead times for roofing and lumber can stretch longer during that window. Humidity year-round means any reconstruction following a water loss has to confirm materials are fully dry before drywall closes the wall back up, or trapped moisture becomes a mold problem months later.
