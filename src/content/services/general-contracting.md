When a kitchen flood or fire leaves you with an opened wall, a stripped subfloor, or cabinets that no longer close right, you're left with a question restoration alone doesn't answer: who rebuilds it, and to what standard? RestorationXpress handles the renovation, remodel, and general contracting side of that work, whether it's a full post-damage rebuild, a standalone kitchen or bathroom remodel, or structural repair that needs a permit before a single stud goes back up.

## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

This work spans two starting points. Some projects begin with damage: a wall opened for water extraction, framing exposed after fire cleanup, or flooring removed during mold remediation that now needs to be replaced and finished. Others start fresh: a homeowner ready to update a dated kitchen, convert a tub to a walk-in shower, or reconfigure a floor plan. Either way, the work touches the same trades: framing, electrical and plumbing rough-in coordination, drywall, cabinetry, tile and flooring, and paint. A bathroom remodel typically runs several weeks from demo to final fixtures; a full kitchen remodel or a structural rebuild after major damage usually takes longer once permitting and inspections are factored in. What matters most on a post-damage job is sequencing: framing and subfloor need to read dry before anything gets closed in, not just dry to the touch.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and written estimate.** Every project starts with a line-item scope, whether it's a kitchen remodel or a rebuild tied to an insurance claim, so you know what's included before demo starts.
2. **Permits and plan review.** Structural changes, electrical and plumbing relocations, and most full remodels require a permit pulled with the local building department before work begins.
3. **Demo and rough-in.** Old materials come out, framing and subfloor get inspected or replaced, and electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in happens before walls close.
4. **Finishes.** Cabinetry, countertops, tile, flooring, trim, and paint go in, matched to existing materials where a repair needs to blend rather than stand out.
5. **Final walkthrough and punch list.** We walk the space with you, note anything that needs adjustment, and close out permits with final inspection.

## What separates a good renovation response from a bad one

The most common mistake on a post-damage rebuild is closing up framing or subfloor before it's actually dry, which traps moisture behind new drywall and sets up a mold problem inside a wall that just got remediated. A good contractor checks moisture content with a meter before cover-up, not by eye. On the remodel side, the usual failure points are skipped permits on work that legally requires one, mismatched materials on a partial repair that make the patch obvious, and change orders that surface mid-project because the original scope was never written down in detail. Insurance adjusters reviewing a rebuild estimate are typically looking for a scope that matches the documented damage, line items that align with standard estimating software, and photos tying each repair back to the loss, not a vague lump-sum number.

## What does renovation and general contracting cost?

Costs vary widely depending on whether the work is a cosmetic update, a full remodel, or a structural rebuild tied to a covered loss, and every project gets a written scope before work begins because no two jobs carry the same labor, permitting, or material needs. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

South Florida's humidity and hurricane season both shape how rebuilds and remodels get specified here. Moisture-resistant drywall and backerboard matter more in Broward County than in drier climates, since standard paper-faced drywall left exposed to humidity swings is more likely to support mold growth. Building code in this region also calls for specific wind-load and impact standards on exterior work, which affects window, door, and roofing scopes tied to storm-damage rebuilds. Permitting volume tends to spike after major storms, so timelines on structural repairs can run longer during those windows simply due to inspection backlogs at the building department.
